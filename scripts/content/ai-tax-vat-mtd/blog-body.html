Making Tax Digital has turned the tax year into four quarterly deadlines for Income Tax and kept VAT on a quarterly cycle. For an accountancy firm, that means more submissions per client, with the same staff. Most of the extra work is reading bank statements and sorting transactions, and this is exactly the work AI agents now do well. In short. An AI agent reads statements and sorts each line into an HMRC category. A rules engine, not the AI, works out the figures, and an accountant approves before anything is filed. Hosted AI costs well under £1 per client per year. A private GPU is a decision about data control, not cost. Build and test in HMRC's sandbox now. Where AI helps in tax and VAT returns

Preparing a return is mostly reading. A landlord with a mixed portfolio can have 275 bank lines in a year. A sole trader mixes business and personal spending on one card. A VAT-registered property business needs a VAT treatment on every line. The judgement is in a small number of lines; the time goes on all the rest.

  • Reading statements. The agent extracts every line from PDF or CSV statements, so nobody types figures in. That also gives you the digital link HMRC requires for VAT: data moved electronically, not copied and pasted.
  • Sorting transactions. Each line is put in an HMRC category using your firm's rules first, then your past decisions for that client, then the model. Lines the agent is unsure of go on a short list for a person.
  • Asking the questions an adviser asks. Is that mortgage residential or commercial? Was that a repair or an improvement? The assistant asks in plain English and records the answer against the figures.
  • Explaining the result. Every figure links back to the transactions behind it, so a reviewer can check a box in seconds.

Where AI should not be used matters just as much. In our builds the AI never calculates tax or the VAT boxes. A rules engine does that, HMRC confirms the Income Tax calculation, and nothing is filed until a person ticks the declaration. HMRC's own guidance is clear that where software automates quarterly updates, the customer is still legally responsible. Your process has to show a person in charge.

What MTD now asks of your clients and your software

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax

  • Who: sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000 (from 6 April 2026), over £30,000 (from 6 April 2027) and over £20,000 (from 6 April 2028). Partnerships follow later. (GOV.UK)
  • What: digital records, cumulative quarterly updates (periods to 5 July, 5 October, 5 January and 5 April, due by the 7th of the second month after), then the tax return. (GOV.UK)
  • Penalties: no points for late quarterly updates in 2026–27; from 2027–28, four points mean a £200 penalty. Late tax returns score points from the start.
  • Software: must keep digital records, send quarterly updates and support the tax return. HMRC lists products that have been through its recognition process in its software finder, and does not recommend any of them. (GOV.UK)

Making Tax Digital for VAT

  • Every VAT-registered business keeps digital records and files through software, with narrow exemptions. (VAT Notice 700/22)
  • Data must move between programs by digital links: import, CSV, file upload or API. Copy and paste does not count.
  • Late returns score penalty points. The threshold is four points for quarterly filers, then £200 for each further late return. (GOV.UK)

Setting up MTD software for an accountancy firm

The accounts your firm needs with HMRC

  • An Agent Services Account (ASA). It is separate from the old agent online services account. You need a Government Gateway user ID, your UTR and approved anti-money-laundering supervision. (GOV.UK)
  • Client authorisation. Existing Self Assessment authorisations carry across. New clients approve you from a request sent by your ASA (the "digital handshake"), and each client is signed up to MTD separately. (GOV.UK)
  • Main or supporting agent. A client has one main agent with full access. Supporting agents can send quarterly updates but cannot submit the return or see calculations, so be clear which you are for each client.

Who is the "software developer" in HMRC's eyes

HMRC deals with whoever owns the production application: the client ID and secret that call its APIs. Its guidance says a developer needs one production application for all its customers, named after the organisation. That gives a firm two routes:

HMRC sandbox: how to test properly, per firm

Every firm should get its own sandbox setup, even on Route A, so you can rehearse your real client mix before anything touches HMRC's live service. What that involves (HMRC testing guide):

  1. A sandbox application on the Developer Hub, subscribed to the APIs you will use. For Income Tax in-year that is Business Details, Obligations, Self-Employment Business and/or Property Business, plus Individual Calculations. End of year adds Business Source Adjustable Summary, Individual Losses and Individuals Tax Liability Adjustments. For VAT, the VAT (MTD) API.
  2. Test users from the Create Test User API: individuals, organisations and an agent, matching your real client types. Unused test users are deleted after 90 days, and an application with no calls for 30 days is deleted, so keep a scheduled smoke test running.
  3. Test scenarios with the Gov-Test-Scenario header: dynamic responses for your own dates, and stateful journeys whose data lasts 7 days. Test the "deny" path of client authorisation, not just "grant".
  4. Fraud prevention headers checked with HMRC's Test Fraud Prevention Headers API. They are a legal requirement, and HMRC warns the validator is not a guarantee, so keep logs of what you send.
  5. A rehearsal of a full quarter for each client type: statements in, review, quarterly update, calculation, then the year-end return. Keep the logs, because HMRC asks for them at production approval.

From sandbox to a real account: approval, security and penetration testing

To move to production, the developer creates a production application, sends its sandbox application ID to HMRC's Software Developer Support Team, completes the Production Approvals Checklist HMRC issues, and HMRC reviews the test logs and header accuracy. (Income Tax end-to-end guide)

Check this before you plan dates. HMRC's guide, updated 15 September 2026, says HMRC "is no longer accepting production credential access requests for new 2026–27 quarterly update products". A new approach is expected in early 2027. Firms that need MTD Income Tax live this season should use Route A with a product HMRC already recognises, or plan Route B for when requests reopen. The sandbox work above is not wasted either way.

The production request includes security questions from HMRC's terms of use. In HMRC's words:

  • "Has your application passed software penetration testing?" You "must test your software for security vulnerabilities before going live", including "regular penetration testing", using "either penetration test tools or an independent third party supplier". HMRC points to the NCSC's penetration testing guidance.
  • "Do you audit security controls to ensure you comply with data protection law?" HMRC points to the ICO's checklist.
  • Further questions cover encryption, keeping customers' data apart, staff access, telling HMRC about a breach, where servers are, your privacy policy and terms, and accessibility (WCAG AA). You have six months to complete the request.

HMRC does not name ISO 27001 or Cyber Essentials. In practice we recommend, for every firm on Route B and for our own Route A builds:

  • an independent penetration test before go-live and at least once a year, covering the web app, the API, sign-in and tenant isolation (one client must never see another's data);
  • a re-test after any major change, such as a new AI model, a new data source or a new hosting location;
  • a data protection impact assessment that names the AI model, where it runs and what it is sent;
  • tested breach notification and access reviews, because HMRC asks about both.

The real question for a firm is where client bank statements are allowed to go. Some firms, and some of their clients, will not allow statements to be sent to a third-party model provider. That is when private GPUs make sense.

Private GPUs: rent or buy

Running an open model on your own GPU keeps statements inside your network or a UK data centre you choose. The model is usually smaller than the best hosted ones, so the rules-first design and the review queue matter more. On our shared test server, a local model took about two hours to read twelve monthly statements. A dedicated modern GPU is much faster, and batch overnight runs suit most firms.

  1. Week 1: choose. Watch the scenarios, list your client types and volumes, and pick Route A or B and hosted or private AI.
