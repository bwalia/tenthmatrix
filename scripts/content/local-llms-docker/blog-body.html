Every time a member of staff pastes a contract, a customer complaint or a spreadsheet into a public AI chatbot, that data leaves your business. Open-source AI models have now become good enough, and small enough, to run on hardware you own. The questions, the documents and the answers stay inside your building. This post explains what that means for a business, what it costs, where it falls short, and the three pieces of software most teams use to do it: Ollama, vLLM and NVIDIA NIM. Developers can go straight to the technical note, which has the Docker set-ups and working examples.

What "local AI" means

A large language model is a file, usually a few gigabytes, plus a program that runs it. Companies such as Meta (Llama), Google (Gemma), Alibaba (Qwen), Mistral and OpenAI (gpt-oss) now publish models whose files anyone can download, which are known as open-weight or open-source models. Once that file is on your own computer or server, you can use it without sending anything to the company that made it. No account, no per-question fee, and no internet connection needed after the download.

To prove the point, we started a model in a Docker container, downloaded it, and then disconnected the container from every network. We asked it why offline AI helps privacy. It answered:

"Running a model without a network connection minimizes the risk of data breaches and unauthorized access because it eliminates the potential for external connections and data transmission."

That was a one-billion-parameter model, small enough for an ordinary laptop, with its network cable effectively cut.

Why it matters: the privacy case

  • Data protection law. Under UK GDPR and the EU GDPR, sending personal data to an AI provider makes them a processor, often in another country. You need contracts, transfer assessments and a clear answer about retention. With a local model, the personal data is processed on your own systems, and that conversation gets much simpler.
  • Client confidentiality. Law firms, accountants, healthcare providers and anyone under a non-disclosure agreement may simply not be allowed to send client material to a third party. Local AI lets those teams use the technology at all.
  • No training on your data. A model on your hardware cannot learn from your prompts and show them to someone else, because nothing is sent anywhere.
  • Data residency. You know exactly which building, and which disk, holds every prompt and answer.
  • A smaller attack surface. No API keys to leak, and no vendor breach that exposes your conversation history.

The three tools, in plain English

Ollama
  • Ollama is the easy one. One command installs it on a laptop, a Mac or a server, and one more downloads a model. It is free and open source (MIT licence) and ideal for a team, an office or a pilot.
  • vLLM is the heavy lifter. It is open source (Apache 2.0) and designed to serve many people at once from powerful NVIDIA graphics cards. Choose it when a local model becomes a service the whole company relies on.
  • NVIDIA NIM packages a model with NVIDIA's own optimisations into a ready-made, supported container. It is free to try for development, and production use is licensed through NVIDIA AI Enterprise. It suits organisations that want a vendor to stand behind the software.

Be honest about the trade-offs

  • The biggest models are still in the cloud. For the hardest reasoning and writing, frontier models such as Claude or GPT are ahead of anything that fits on a desk. Many businesses use both: local for sensitive or high-volume work, cloud for the rest.
  • Hardware matters. Small models run on a modern laptop. Larger models want a workstation with plenty of graphics memory, or a server. Serving a whole company needs proper GPUs.
  • Someone has to look after it. Updates, monitoring, backups and access control become your job, or your partner's.
  • Local is not automatically safe. A model on your network still needs access controls, and an AI agent that can run code still needs a sandbox. See our posts on OpenShell and agent safety.
  • Check the licence. "Open" models come with different licences. Most allow commercial use, but some add conditions.

Where businesses start

  1. A private chat assistant for staff who handle client data: summarising, drafting and translating without anything leaving the office.
  2. Redacting before sharing: a local model strips names, emails and phone numbers from text before it goes anywhere else, including to a cloud model.
  3. Document search over contracts, policies or manuals, answered with citations.
  4. Bulk sorting: tagging support tickets, invoices or CVs by the thousand at no per-item cost.
  5. Developer tools: code assistants that never see your source code leave the building.
